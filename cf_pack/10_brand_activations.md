## A. The Crowd-Crafted Christmas Ad

One sentence: after two straight years of "AI slop" backlash on its AI-generated Christmas ads, the brand's next holiday ad is made BY its fans' hands, with AI only helping them design, and the submissions ARE the ad.

- The wound: Coca-Cola's AI caravan ads drew boycott-level backlash in 2024 and 2025; McDonald's NL pulled its AI Christmas ad within days. Brand teams now need an AI story where humans visibly made the thing.
- The mechanic: brand kit (bottle, truck, Santa, palette) in a walled Studio flow; fans design ornaments, papercraft, knits with AI assist, physically make them, film a 5-second clip of the object. The brand's holiday spot is a stop-motion montage of thousands of real crafted objects, credited to their makers; the best sit on the Times Square billboard slot the brand already uses (Create Real Magic precedent).
- Why it is clever: the campaign is itself the answer to the brand's specific crisis. The UGC is not promotion for the ad; it IS the ad. The press line writes itself: "the Christmas ad AI could not make alone, so ten thousand people made it."
- Why only CF: needs a platform whose users finish things physically, at volume, with moderation and licensing rails. Freepik and Higgsfield end at the screen.
- KPI: makes submitted, tracked sign-ups from the campaign page, then the ad's own earned reach as the brand's currency.
- Timing honesty: a broadcast spot needs a long lead, so the 2026 version is the billboard/social cut and the gallery; the TV ad is the 2027 renewal. Say this unprompted.
- Target brands: Coca-Cola first (they own the wound and the Create Real Magic infrastructure), Heinz as alternate (proven AI-campaign buyer, packaging canvas).

## B. The Fan Merch Amnesty

One sentence: every hit show or game spawns thousands of unlicensed fan-merch listings on Etsy that the studio's lawyers play whack-a-mole with; license the fans instead, through CF, and take a royalty.

- The wound: IP owners spend real money on takedowns against their own superfans, and the fans hate them for it. The demand is proven by the infringement itself.
- The mechanic: the studio licenses an official asset pack into CF Studio and the marketplace for one IP, one product category, one season window. Sellers who design from the pack can sell fan merch legally; the royalty (LEGO Ideas charges fans 1% the other way; here the studio takes its cut) is automatic in CF's checkout; CF moderates and reports. Everything outside the pack stays enforceable as before.
- Why it is clever: it converts an enforcement cost line into a royalty revenue line and a goodwill headline ("[Studio] licenses its fans"). No one else can run it: it needs creation tools, a seller marketplace, moderation, and licensing rails in one place, which is exactly CF and only CF.
- KPI: sellers onboarded, licensed listings live, royalty run rate, plus tracked sign-ups of new sellers who came for the licence.
- Risk and containment: licensing legal is heavy, so the pitch is a contained pilot: one IP, one category (say ornaments or apparel), one quarter. The Q4 2026/Q1 2027 tentpole slate (Avengers Doomsday 18 Dec, Shrek 5, Narnia) gives the shortlist.
- Target: a studio consumer-products team already comfortable with partner slates (Netflix's next tentpole; or a game publisher, where fan-merch enforcement is loudest).

## C. Launch the Colour Like a Model

One sentence: Pantone launches a colour once a year the way AI labs launch models; treat Color of the Year 2027 as a day-one model drop, and every Studio tool speaks the colour the hour it is announced.

- The insight: CF's core competence (the day-one launch playbook from Concept 1) is an engine, not a Kling-specific trick. Pantone's CotY reveal in early December is an embargoed launch with a hungry audience and no maker platform attached.
- The mechanic: Pantone gives CF the palette under embargo; at announcement hour every relevant Studio tool and template pack is pre-tuned to the colour; a 48-hour "first makes" challenge follows; Pantone reposts the wall of physical makes as its proof the colour landed in culture on day one.
- Why it is clever: Pantone's whole business is convincing the world a colour matters; a wall of real objects in that colour within 24 hours is the best evidence money can buy, and it costs Pantone almost nothing (a palette and reposts). For CF it is the launch playbook running on a non-AI partner, which is exactly the "repeatable engine" the JD asks for.
- Why only CF: Adobe and Canva can render the colour; only CF's users cut, print, stitch and ship it.
- KPI: the CEO's units on the challenge page; day-one makes; press pickups of the wall.
- Timing: CotY 2027 announcement is early December 2026, inside the window; the ask is small enough to close in weeks.
- Target: Pantone licensing (they license CotY commercially, so the conversation is native to them).

## The improv answer (keep loaded, do not present)

If Roemie replays his Pringles test live: the tube. Crafters already upcycle Pringles cans; it is an existing craft meme. "Don't recycle it, make it": AI-designed tube wraps and cut patterns in Studio turn every empty can into a crafted object posted with the flavour launch hashtag. The product's own packaging becomes the craft supply, which is the right angle for any brand whose package has a shape people keep.

## Why these three beat "Make the Gift"

"Make the Gift" was structurally sound but generic: licensed assets plus gifts plus UGC could be pitched by any platform. Each of these three is anchored in a pain or a moment that belongs to the brand (the AI backlash, the enforcement cost, the launch date) and can only be executed by CF (physical output, seller marketplace, launch engine). That is the difference between a campaign idea and a partnership idea, and it is the thing the assessment exists to test.
