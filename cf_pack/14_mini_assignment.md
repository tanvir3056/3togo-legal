## What I took from the conversation

Two things stood out.

First, partnerships here exist to reduce dependence on paid acquisition and build distribution for Studio. You said paid is a large share of how Creative Fabrica acquires users and that it is expensive; partnerships are the way you want to break from it.

Second, this is not conventional sales, and you said so directly. The job is finding moments where CF, a model provider or a consumer brand can create something users actively want to make and share. Your Pringles example is the shape: the brand supplies the moment, the users make the thing, and both sides get the attention.

What follows are the three concepts I would test first, who I would contact, and the first 30 days.

## How I prioritised

You gave me the filters yourself. Consumer partnerships are for awareness and acquisition, not sales. There has to be some way to quantify it. Paid is expensive, so a partnership only counts if it brings people CF would otherwise pay for. And your working bar is roughly 20,000 users in 14 days for a feature push, so speed matters as much as size. I use that bar throughout. Five criteria:

| Criterion | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| User pull | Would a normal consumer actually want to make or share this? |
| Partner incentive | Does the partner have an obvious reason to say yes? |
| Studio fit | Does Studio materially enable it, or could anyone run it? |
| Distribution | Does it bring users beyond CF's existing audience? |
| Speed and measurability | Can it launch fast and be measured cleanly? |

Scores are 1 to 5 and deliberately coarse. I trust the ordering, not the individual points. Two scores worth flagging: model launches only get a 3 on distribution because day-one availability is shared with three competitors (more in concept one). The agency and event routes score 1 on user pull because no consumer makes anything.

## Concept one: turn model launches into consumer campaigns

### Opportunity

Freepik, fal and Krea get the same new models on day one and publish similar demo content. Day-one availability stopped being a differentiator the moment three competitors matched it. The speed you already have: you said models can land with hours of notice and your team ships them within a day.

What the others do not have is your users. CF users are crafters, makers and small sellers. They make physical things and photograph them, because photographs are how they sell. When a new video model drops, Freepik posts another polished demo clip. A CF user can point the same model at a quilt she finished yesterday. That is the asset to sell to model providers: an audience that applies new models to real objects, at volume, in launch week.

### Activation

One campaign format, built once and rerun at every launch: Bring Your Make To Life. A user uploads a photo of something they made or something they sell. The new video model animates it. A knitted dragon breathes fire; a seller's product photo becomes a short promo video for her shop.

The point of the format is repeatability. The landing page, entry flow, prompt pack and tracking stay the same; only the model name, the capability and the example clips change. Each launch looks like a fresh campaign from the outside and is a re-skin internally.

### Why the provider cares

Providers compete to be first and noticed. First is now shared with three competitors. Noticed is not.

| What they get | Why it is different |
|---|---|
| Real-world use cases | Every competitor will post cinematic samples; none of the day-one competitors has an audience of makers photographing physical things, so CF is uniquely positioned to show the model animating hand-made objects |
| Generation volume | A challenge drives generations in the first days, exactly when the provider wants usage numbers |
| Creator adoption | Participants are makers with shops and social accounts, and they post what they make |
| Launch-week visibility | Shared content lands while the provider's own launch push is running |

To be clear about what I am claiming: none of this is a committed co-marketing deal. It is a pitch, and it needs the provider conversation to become one. But it costs the provider nothing and answers a launch-week problem they visibly have, which is why I think the conversation is winnable.

### Why CF cares

A model launch stops being a feature announcement and becomes an acquisition event. To take part, a user has to sign up and generate. That is the whole funnel: see a maker's dragon in motion, click through, create an account, animate your own thing. Every launch reuses the same template, so cost per campaign falls after the first run. Signups arrive through a mechanic CF owns, which points away from paid. And it gives providers a reason to prioritise CF beyond implementation speed: speed gets you the model, the campaign format gets you noticed with it.

### The launch kit

Built in advance, so a launch with hours of notice can still go live fast.

| Component | What it is |
|---|---|
| Landing page template | One page per launch: model name, capability demo, entry button. Cloned and re-skinned each time |
| Challenge mechanic | Upload your make, generate, share. Simple rules, one tag per launch |
| Prompt and template pack | Pre-built prompts per capability so a first-time user gets a good result on the first attempt |
| Tracking | Tagged links and a signup source flag per campaign, set up before launch |
| Moderation rules | Written once: what gets featured, what gets removed, rights to reshare user content |
| Launch assets | Announcement copy, example clips, email to existing users, templated with slots for model name and capability |
| Channel plan | Which owned channels fire, in which order, in the first 48 hours |

Fallback: assets are built per capability rather than per date. If a release slips, the same package waits for the next model with that capability and nothing is wasted. Given the notice window, the campaign has to be date-independent by design.

Inside each provider, four functions matter: model or product partnerships, developer ecosystem, growth partnerships, and product marketing. Product marketing owns launch-week visibility, so the pitch aims there first. The specific routes are in the contact list below.

### Measurement

| Metric | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| Tracked signups | New accounts through the campaign landing page, source-tagged |
| First generation rate | A signup that never generates is not an activated user |
| Share rate | Share of generators who post the result; the number the provider cares about |
| Repeat usage at 7 and 14 days | Whether the campaign brought makers or drive-by clickers |
| Cost per activated user | Reported only if paid support boosts the campaign; an organic run has no paid line |

I would hold the first run against the 20,000 in 14 days bar rather than invent a separate target. Then treat it as the baseline the second run has to beat. Main dependency: the timing of the next release, possibly the Kling launch. Earliest proof point: tracked signups in the first 14 days of the first wrapped launch.

## Concept two: Kid's Drawing Made Real

The strongest version of the outbound approach you described. The trigger is the one piece of user content every family already produces and keeps: a child's drawing.

### Consumer behaviour

A child draws something. A parent photographs the drawing and uploads it. Studio returns an animation of the drawing, plus optional outputs: a colouring page, a printable, a pattern, or a keepsake file ready to print.

The engine is the before-and-after share: the original drawing beside the transformed result. The product explains itself in one image, the child is the visible author. Every parent who sees it knows exactly what to do with the drawing on their own fridge. The partner brand sits inside the frame of every share.

### Why this is strong

- **IKEA has proven the input at scale.** SAGOSKATT has run since 2014: kids submit drawings of their dream soft toy, tens of thousands of entries a year. Winning drawings become real plush sold worldwide, with proceeds to children's charities. Families will hand a brand their children's drawings in volume.
- **Meta has proven the transformation, then walked away.** Its free Animated Drawings research tool animates children's drawings and was widely used, but Meta never productised it. The demand is demonstrated and the product slot is empty.
- **Budsies has proven willingness to pay.** 76,000+ custom plush toys made from kids' drawings at roughly 99 dollars each. If parents pay that for one physical version, a free branded digital version has obvious pull.
- The creativity on display is visibly the child's; the AI only executes it. The concept carries none of the backlash risk brands face when AI generates content from nothing.

### Outreach thesis

No email drafts, just the one sentence each pitch hangs on.

**Crayola:** Turn every child's drawing into an animated creation inside a Crayola-branded Studio experience. Families get a reason to come back and share; Crayola extends from art materials into AI-assisted creativity.

**HEMA:** Give every Dutch family a knutsel moment that ends with the child's drawing coming to life on screen and printed as a HEMA keepsake. The craft shelf becomes the start of something families share, not the end of a purchase.

### Measurement

Every partner gets a distinct entry point (co-branded link or code) so all numbers attribute cleanly. The metrics mirror concept one: participants through the partner route, signup to generation conversion, before-and-after shares and prints, and return rate at 14 days. Return rate is what separates a stunt from an acquisition channel. One addition: partner amplification, the reach of the partner's own posts and placements, the earned media that replaces paid spend. A Tier 1 pilot is scoped and reported against the 20,000 in 14 days bar, and no revenue line appears anywhere, because the job is awareness. No dependency to start: CF can run the pilot alone and a brand adds reach later. Earliest proof point: the share rate of before-and-after posts in a two week pilot.

## Concept three: Official Creator Challenge

The outbound approach turned into a product. A brand supplies a moment, consumers make and share something with it, and every new brand is a configuration of the same build. The Stranger Things crafts collaboration already showed both sides get enough out of it: a lot of attention, a headline each. This turns that one-off into a calendar.

The split of work stays the same every time:

| Who | Provides |
|---|---|
| Brand | Approved asset pack (characters, sigils, product elements, typography), a theme, amplification commitment |
| CF | Generation environment in Studio, campaign templates, moderation, co-branded landing page, tracking |
| Consumers | The entries: shorts, reels, designs built from the pack |
| Brand | Reposts selected work on its own channels |

Every challenge gets one URL, so entries, creators and new accounts are countable per campaign and readable against the 20,000 in 14 days bar.

You said almost any brand works if you find the right angle. In this format the angle is always three questions. Is there a moment? Is there an asset pack the brand can legally approve? Is there a mechanic a consumer finishes in one sitting? Three verticals pass right now.

| Vertical | Campaign | Window | Mechanic | Hard constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Food and beverage | Fanta Haunted Universe | Now to Halloween | 30 second haunted shorts from the official character pack | Ghost Face element excluded (third-party IP) |
| Entertainment | Dune Part Three | Opens 18 December 2026 | Hype films from an official sigil and typography pack | No actor likenesses, ever |
| Gaming | Call of Duty MW4 | Releases 23 October 2026 | Squad hype reels from a licensed emblem pack | Needs a Microsoft/Activision licence |

**Fanta.** Haunted Universe launched in late August 2026 across 50 markets, built on original horror characters Fanta owns, and Fanta says it wants Halloween to become "Fanta's Christmas". An annual event needs a participation format, and its first big Halloween window under this IP is open now. Owner on their side: Fanta's brand digital team.

**Dune.** 18 December is the day fans already call "Dunesday": Dune Part Three and Avengers Doomsday open against each other, so the studio needs every bit of fan noise in the weeks before. The pack design handles the likeness rule up front. Owner: the studio's international digital marketing function.

**Call of Duty.** An annual franchise launch, so a campaign that works once runs again every year. It starts with the licensing function rather than the marketing team. GTA VI on 19 November 2026 is the bigger moment, but Rockstar bans commercial fan use, so it stays a stretch case only.

### Rights and brand safety

Same rules on every challenge, agreed before launch:

- Approved asset packs only; nothing enters the generation environment the brand has not cleared.
- No unlicensed character or likeness generation, full stop.
- All entries run in a moderated tier; human review before anything is officially featured.
- Brand approval workflow for any piece the partner reposts.
- Written campaign usage terms: what the brand may use, where, and for how long.

Dependency: the first brand's asset pack and approval workflow. Earliest proof point: one signed pilot with entries submitted and signups tracked back to the campaign.

## First 30 days

Four tracks, run in parallel from day one. Not a day-by-day calendar, because model drops do not respect calendars.

### 1. Model launch pipeline

A live provider map, kept current weekly: contact route, expected releases, integration status, and the launch opportunity attached to each. Google first: formalise an early heads-up channel so drops arrive with days of notice instead of hours. Kling next: if the launch you flagged lands inside the 30 days, it becomes the first live run of the kit. ByteDance and Alibaba routes get mapped in week one so the format never depends on one provider's roadmap.

Integration status is the easy column; engineering is not the constraint. So each priority model type gets a pre-built launch kit: three seeded maker briefs, a prepared landing page, tracking links, ready before the model lands. Whether maker-made content outperforms demo reels is measured.

### 2. Consumer partnership pipeline

One gate rule before any outreach: every target gets a concrete activation, a metric and a draft landing page attached first. The metric: attributed sign-ups, entries and shares, defined before the first email goes out.

The first wave is the contact list above: Fanta, M&M's, Crayola, HEMA, IKEA and BBC Studios, each through the doors listed there. One addition from the workbook: Sesame Workshop, through Kendall Hubert, EVP Revenue and Philanthropy, for the drawings-to-real-objects mechanic with a charity component. A revenue-plus-philanthropy remit is the right door for the SAGOSKATT shape.

The second wave is sequenced by calendar and licence lead time.

- The Call of Duty licence conversation only makes sense if it starts now.
- Percy Jackson runs weekly on Disney+ from 20 November.
- Eurovision national finals run January to March 2027.
- Ravensburger enters once its licensing contact is confirmed.

Two Amsterdam items also fall inside the 30 days. One is an intro with DEPT, which launched an open AI orchestration layer this year built to plug in external tools and has a dedicated Creative Automation practice. The other is a decision on World Summit AI before the partner window closes.

### 3. Reusable campaign kit

Built once in the first two weeks, then reused by everything above. The second campaign should cost a fraction of the first.

- **Landing page template.** Brand block, challenge brief, entry gallery, sign-up. Reskinned per campaign in hours.
- **Tracking.** Per-campaign links and codes so each partner sees numbers that are unambiguously theirs.
- **Moderation process.** Human review before any entry goes public, plus a takedown path. Non-negotiable, because half this pipeline touches children's art.
- **Challenge rules.** Standard entry terms, IP and usage rights, age handling, winner selection. Written once with counsel, reused everywhere.
- **Brand asset intake checklist.** Files, usage restrictions, approval contact, turnaround commitments, so requirements like a full character pack surface before launch week.
- **Post-campaign measurement template.** One page: entries, new users, cost, what the partner published, and a keep or kill call.

You said this starts from a laptop with no playbook. This kit is how the playbook gets written, by shipping.

### 4. Small tests

Small tests, measured signal, repeat or kill. None need a partner's sign-off, which is the point: they back the pitches above with evidence from our own audience.

1. **Owned Halloween craft challenge** on the existing audience, no brand attached. Proves the campaign kit end to end before any partner sees it.
2. **Kids' drawing to finished object.** The external demand evidence is in concept two; what is unknown is whether CF's audience participates and shares. That is the test.
3. **Pet portrait style pack.** Chewy has sent 1,000+ hand-painted pet portraits a week to customers for about a decade, so demand for pet art is proven. Test whether it pulls new users into Studio with zero IP risk.
4. **Launch format A/B on the next model drop.** Maker-shot physical results against standard demo content, measured on pickup and sign-ups.

These first tests are not sized to hit the 20,000 in 14 days bar and I will not pretend otherwise. They are sized to show whether a mechanic produces attributable sign-ups at all. Every test gets a written keep or kill decision within a week of closing; whatever shows signal is repeated bigger.

### What exists on day 30

- Provider map live, all four inbound routes opened or mapped, and one launch shipped through the kit if a model drops in the window.
- First outreach out to the openers, each with activation, metric and draft page attached.
- Campaign kit used at least once for real and revised once.
- Three or more test results with keep or kill decisions on paper.
